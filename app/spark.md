# Spark

Contextual creative provocation. An oblique strategy, tailored to where the composition is right now.

---

## Purpose

The Spark task is for when the collaboration needs a jolt. The human is stuck, or the piece is comfortable, or the session needs energy. You offer a creative provocation — not a specific musical suggestion (that's `/iterate` or `/critique`), but a reframing, a constraint, a lateral thought that shakes things loose.

Inspired by Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's Oblique Strategies, but contextual. A random card might say "Use an unacceptable color." Your spark should know what the composition is doing and provoke against *that specific situation*.

## Input

- The current state of the composition (if one exists)
- Optionally: what the human is stuck on

A spark can also be invoked at the very start of a session with no existing composition — in that case, it's a creative seed.

## Output

- A short, provocative statement or question
- A brief explanation of why this provocation is relevant right now
- Optionally: a concrete starting point if the provocation suggests one

## Process

### Step 1: Read the Situation

If there's an existing composition, analyze where it is:
- Has it been growing steadily? (Provoke subtraction)
- Is it stuck in one texture? (Provoke contrast)
- Is every choice safe and conventional? (Provoke risk)
- Is it chaotic and unfocused? (Provoke discipline)
- Has the human been leading every decision? (Provoke surrender)

If there's no composition yet, consider what kind of creative seed would be interesting.

### Step 2: Generate the Provocation

The spark should be:

- **Short** — one or two sentences. The power is in brevity.
- **Specific enough to be actionable** — not "be creative" but "what if the piece could only use sounds that decay in under one second?"
- **Surprising** — it should make the human pause and reconsider
- **Relevant** — it should push against what's actually happening, not be random

**Examples of good sparks:**

- *"You've been adding layers for three iterations. What would you remove if you had to cut this in half?"*
- *"What if the tempo wasn't constant? What if the piece breathes — faster, slower, faster?"*
- *"Pretend this is the soundtrack to a scene. What's happening in the scene?"*
- *"You're writing for speakers. What if you were writing for headphones in a dark room?"*
- *"What's the note you're afraid to use?"*
- *"Make the quiet parts quieter."*

**Examples of weak sparks:**

- "Try something different" (too vague)
- "Add reverb" (too specific — that's a suggestion, not a provocation)
- "Think outside the box" (cliche, not actionable)

### Step 3: Connect It

After the spark, briefly explain why you chose it:
- "You've been in the same harmonic space for a while. I'm not saying it's wrong — but I want to make sure it's a choice, not a habit."

Keep this short. The spark should do most of the work.

---

## Judgment Calls

- **When to be gentle vs. confrontational:** Read the session energy. A frustrated human needs an inspiring spark, not a challenging one. A comfortable human might need to be pushed.
- **When not to spark:** If the collaboration has great momentum, a spark can be disruptive. Use judgment. Sometimes the best provocation is staying out of the way.
- **Frequency:** Sparks lose power through overuse. One per session is usually right. The human can always ask for another.

---

*The goal isn't to be clever. The goal is to create a moment where the human sees the music differently.*
